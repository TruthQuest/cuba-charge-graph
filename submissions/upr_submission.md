## 1. Introduction

This submission focuses on a single systemic issue in Cuba's criminal justice system: the practice of assigning political defendants to one of two disjoint prosecutorial charging regimes prior to any individualized assessment of the acts alleged. The pattern is documented by statistical analysis of the case files of 1,258 political prisoners covering the period 2021 to 2026. This submission argues that the practice constitutes a form of arbitrary detention and selective prosecution inconsistent with Cuba's obligations under the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR) and customary international law as recognized by the WGAD in Deliberation No. 9, and the recommendations Cuba accepted at its Fourth Cycle review in May 2023.

The submission is offered independently. I have no affiliation with the Government of Cuba, with any Cuban opposition organization, or with any foreign government. I am the sole author.

## 2. Background: methodology and source

The analysis rests on the publicly available political-prisoner registry maintained by Prisoners Defenders (Madrid), a civil society organization that has documented Cuban political detention continuously since 2018. The registry lists 1,258 individuals as of May 2026, with charges, sentences, arrest dates, and detention locations for each where public information permits. Prisoners Defenders is the primary source. I encoded the registry into a formal ontology and applied statistical analysis. Prisoners Defenders has been informed of this submission.

The statistical method is a permutation test: the observed co-occurrence of charge pairs is compared to a null distribution generated by 5,000 iterations of random charge assignment that preserves per-defendant charge count and per-charge overall frequency. Results reported below have empirical p-values below 0.001 unless otherwise stated. The full pipeline is versioned and reproducible; interested parties can verify every quantitative claim.

## 3. Implementation of recommendations from Cuba's Fourth Cycle (2023)

At its Fourth Cycle review in May 2023, Cuba received recommendations from numerous states concerning freedom of expression, arbitrary detention, and judicial independence. Cuba accepted a number of recommendations concerning economic and social rights and rejected or "noted" the substantial majority of recommendations concerning political rights, freedom of assembly, and treatment of persons detained in connection with the July 2021 demonstrations.

The recommendations Cuba noted included calls to:

- Release persons detained solely for exercising their rights to freedom of expression, association, and peaceful assembly.
- Bring domestic criminal law into conformity with UDHR Articles 19 (freedom of expression), 20 (peaceful assembly), and 21 (right to participate in government).
- Establish independent oversight of the prosecutorial function and judicial appointments.
- Cease the practice of overbroad charging in political cases.

None of the above has been implemented. The pattern documented in this submission indicates that in the period since the 2023 review, the practice of overbroad and pre-sorted charging has continued and, in the case of the December 2022 Penal Code (Law No. 151), has been institutionally consolidated.

## 4. Domestic legal framework

The 2019 Cuban Constitution designates the Communist Party of Cuba as the "superior driving force of the society and the State" (Article 5). Judges of the People's Supreme Court and provincial courts are elected and removable by the National Assembly of People's Power (Articles 148, 149), a body in which no non-Communist political party can lawfully organize. The Fiscalía General de la República reports to the National Assembly (Article 156). No independent bar association exists. No mechanism exists for independent review of prosecutorial charging decisions.

The 2022 Penal Code, which entered into force on 1 December 2022, criminalizes political expression through a set of overbroad provisions:

- **Article 120 (Sedition):** 7 to 15 years, or death in aggravated cases
- **Article 143 (Propaganda against the Constitutional Order):** 4 to 10 years
- **Article 144 (Enemy Propaganda):** 1 to 8 years
- **Article 185 (Contempt):** 6 months to 3 years
- **Article 272 (Public Disorder):** 3 months to 1 year
- **Article 274 (Resistance):** 3 months to 1 year
- **Article 276 (Assault on Authority):** variable

These provisions are drafted with sufficient breadth that a single act of political expression can independently satisfy the elements of multiple offenses. The 2022 code retained the substantive scope of the 1987 code's political provisions while raising penalties and extending the reach of certain articles to online conduct.

## 5. The evidence: bifurcated charging as a systemic practice

Analysis of the 2,264 charges filed against the 1,258 individuals in the corpus reveals two entirely disjoint co-occurrence clusters. The clustering is not consistent with charging decisions arising from acts alleged.

### 5.1 Regime A: the street-protest bundle

Charges from the low-severity political offense catalogue (Public Disorder, Contempt, Assault, Resistance) co-occur at rates 2.3x to 3.4x above what independent random assignment would produce:

- Contempt + Public Disorder: observed 288, expected under null 94 (p < 0.001)
- Assault + Public Disorder: observed 253, expected 88 (p < 0.001)
- Assault + Contempt: observed 175, expected 63 (p < 0.001)
- Public Disorder + Resistance: observed 30, expected 11 (p < 0.001)

This bundle is applied to persons detained in connection with public gatherings, expressive conduct, or protest activity. It functions to multiply sentence exposure by combining low-severity offenses whose elements substantially overlap in the same alleged conduct.

### 5.2 Regime B: the standalone regime charge

The Sedition charge (Article 120) is filed in 218 cases. In 199 of those cases, no charge from Regime A appears on the same charge sheet:

- Sedition + Public Disorder: observed 10, expected 57 (empirical p = 1.000; the observed value is well below the 5th percentile of the null distribution)
- Sedition + Assault: observed 9, expected 38 (p = 1.000)

Under a charging practice tied to the acts alleged, one would expect Sedition to co-occur with Public Disorder in roughly one-quarter of cases, since acts against the constitutional order almost by definition occur in the context of public gatherings that would satisfy the elements of Public Disorder. The near-total absence is inconsistent with charging based on acts and consistent with pre-sorting based on the state's characterization of the defendant.

### 5.3 The consequence

The state assigns political defendants to one of two prosecutorial regimes prior to trial and without individualized assessment. Defendants sorted into Regime A face sentence-multiplying bundles of low-severity charges. Defendants sorted into Regime B face grave standalone charges carrying 7 to 15 years. The sorting is not disclosed in any statute, not reasoned in any judgment, and not disclosed to defense counsel. The pattern is stable across the five-year observation window and across every Cuban province.



Independent validation via Louvain community detection (Blondel et al. 2008) confirms the two-regime partition. An unsupervised algorithm, given only the charge co-occurrence graph and no domain knowledge, independently recovered the same structure with 100% stability across 100 random seeds and 97-98% prisoner-level classification agreement.

## 6. Individual cases illustrating the pattern

The following individuals from the dataset are already publicly identified, are already the subject of prior international reporting, and are named here to illustrate the pattern:

**Maykel Castillo Pérez** ("Maykel Osorbo"), co-writer of "Patria y Vida" (winner of two Latin Grammy Awards, 2021). Detained May 2021. Convicted June 2022 on charges including Contempt, Assault, Public Disorder, and Defamation of Institutions. Sentence: nine years. His case is documented in Human Rights Watch's *Prison or Exile* (2022) and in Amnesty International reporting. His charge sheet is the archetypal Regime A bundle.

**Luis Manuel Otero Alcántara**, founder of the San Isidro Movement. Detained 11 July 2021. Convicted 2022 on charges including Contempt, Public Disorder, and outrage against national symbols. Sentence: five years. Regime A pattern. Prior beneficiary of Inter-American Commission precautionary measures (Resolution 21/2020).

**José Daniel Ferrer**, founder of the Unión Patriótica de Cuba. Detained repeatedly since 2003. His case history illustrates the pattern of migration from Regime A charging in earlier detentions to Regime B framing in more recent ones. Beneficiary of Inter-American Commission precautionary measures (Resolution 39/2020).

The full corpus classified by regime is available in the accompanying materials.

## 7. Legal analysis

The pattern engages Cuba's obligations under the UDHR and customary international law. Cuba has signed but not ratified the ICCPR, making ICCPR-based arguments legally moot. The following analysis routes through UDHR provisions and Deliberation No. 9 of the WGAD:

**UDHR Article 10 (Right to a fair hearing):** Article 10 guarantees a fair hearing by a competent, independent, and impartial tribunal. A charging practice made by an office not independent of the executive, tried before a tribunal not independent of that office, without disclosure to defense counsel of the criteria applied in charging, is inconsistent with the fair-trial guarantee. Article 14(2) guarantees the presumption of innocence, which is inconsistent with the pre-sorting of defendants into regimes with materially different sentence exposures before trial.

**UDHR Article 3 (Right to life, liberty and security):** Article 3, read together with Article 9 (prohibition of arbitrary detention), prohibits arbitrary detention. Detention on the basis of a charging decision not disclosed in law and not reviewable by any independent body is arbitrary within the meaning of the Human Rights Committee's General Comment No. 35.

**UDHR Article 7 (Equality before the law):** The differential treatment of defendants through assignment to distinct prosecutorial regimes on criteria not disclosed in the record engages the guarantee of equal protection of the law without discrimination on grounds including political opinion.

The Human Rights Committee has previously found similar patterns of pre-sorting and selective charging inconsistent with the Covenant in its Concluding Observations on other States Parties. The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention has consistently applied a similar analysis under Categories III (fair trial violations of such gravity as to render detention arbitrary) and V (detention constituting discrimination on political grounds) of its jurisprudence.

## 8. Recommendations to the Government of Cuba

I recommend that the Government of Cuba be encouraged, in the recommendations issued at the Fifth Cycle:

1. **Repeal or substantially narrow** Articles 120, 143, 144, 185, 272, 274, and 276 of the 2022 Penal Code, or, in the alternative, adopt authoritative interpretive guidance sharply constraining their application to acts of expression and peaceful assembly.

2. **Publish, and require prosecutors to apply,** written charging guidelines that require charges to be tied to specific alleged acts and require charging decisions to be reasoned on the record and disclosed to defense counsel.

3. **Establish an independent mechanism** for review of political-offense charging decisions prior to trial, staffed by persons not accountable to the Fiscalía General or the National Assembly.

4. **Release, or grant new individualized proceedings to,** the individuals in the Prisoners Defenders registry whose current detention rests on charge combinations characteristic of the bifurcated pattern documented herein.

5. **Ratify the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights** and accept the individual communications procedure under its First Optional Protocol, and accept the competence of the Committee against Torture to receive individual communications under Article 22 of the Convention against Torture.

6. **Grant standing invitations** to the UN Special Rapporteur on the promotion and protection of the right to freedom of opinion and expression, the Special Rapporteur on human rights defenders, and the Special Rapporteur on the independence of judges and lawyers.

7. **Provide the Inter-American Commission on Human Rights** with unimpeded access to Cuban detention facilities for the purpose of monitoring individuals identified in prior Commission resolutions on precautionary measures.

## 9. Recommendations to States participating in the review

I recommend that States participating in the Fifth Cycle:

1. **Refer** the pattern documented in this submission in their statements and recommendations to the Government of Cuba, both because the pattern itself is significant and because doing so signals that quantitative evidence of systemic practice is now available and will be examined in future cycles.

2. **Request** that the Government of Cuba report, in its national report for the Fifth Cycle, on the criteria applied by the Fiscalía General in selecting between low-severity and high-severity political-offense charges, and provide statistics on the ratio of charges filed by category over the review period.

3. **Support** civil society access to the review through direct engagement with Prisoners Defenders, the Cuban Observatory for Human Rights (OCDH), Cubalex, and other organizations documenting political detention in Cuba, and treat their case-level evidence as complementary to the systemic evidence presented in this submission.
